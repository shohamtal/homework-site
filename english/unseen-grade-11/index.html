Should Teenagers Have a Four-Day School Week?

Every few years, someone suggests that schools should open only four days a week, and every time the idea is rejected. In my view, it is time to take it . Several school districts in the United States have already tried a four-day week, mainly to save money on buses, heating and cleaning. In most of these districts, the school day became about an hour longer, so students did not lose any learning time. The results were . Teachers reported that improved, because students used the free day for doctor's appointments and family matters instead of missing lessons. Many schools also found it easier to good teachers, who valued the extra day for planning and rest. To my mind, however, the strongest argument is not about money or attendance. It is about what teenagers do with their time. A free weekday gives young people a chance to work on a project they care about, to volunteer, or simply to from a busy week. This is a fair concern, and any school that changes its week must offer activities or for families who need it. Others worry that longer days will be too for younger pupils, whose attention fades in the afternoon. Some research also suggests that test results in maths dropped slightly in a few districts after the change. These are real problems, but they are not reasons to ignore the idea completely. Instead, they are reasons to plan it carefully. A school could, for example, keep the fifth day for clubs, sports and extra help, so that it becomes a different kind of learning day rather than a day off. Why Octopuses Are So Clever

If you were asked to name an intelligent animal, you would probably think of a chimpanzee, a dolphin or perhaps a dog. Few people would ever choose a creature without a single bone anywhere in its body. Yet scientists now believe that the octopus is one of the most intelligent animals on Earth. An octopus has about five hundred million , roughly as many as a dog. What makes it unusual is where those cells are. Only around a third of them are in its brain, while the rest are spread along its eight arms. This means that each arm can, in a sense, think for itself. An arm can taste, touch and explore a rock while the brain is busy with something completely different. Researchers have watched octopuses solve problems that would challenge many . In laboratory , they have learned to open jars with screw tops in order to reach a crab inside. Some have even escaped from their tanks at night, crossed the floor to another tank, eaten a fish and returned before morning. In the wild, one species collects coconut shells and carries them across the sea floor, later using them as a shelter. This kind of planning ahead was once thought to be to humans and apes. Octopuses are also masters of . Special cells in their skin allow them to change colour and texture in less than a second, so that they into sand, coral or seaweed. Strangely, they seem to do this even though their eyes probably cannot see colour, and scientists are still how they manage it. Why did such a clever animal in the sea? One popular suggests that losing their shell, millions of years ago, made octopuses . Without protection, they had to survive by being smarter than the fish that hunted them. There is, however, a sad side to their story. Most octopuses live for only one or two years, and they never meet their parents, so almost everything they know must be learned alone. Some researchers find this the most fact of all: a lifetime of learning squeezed into a few short seasons. In short, the more closely we study octopuses, the more they challenge our idea of what intelligence looks like.
